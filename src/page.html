How to take a good reading

  1. Same time each day, ideally soon after waking, before coffee. Lie flat on your back on the bed.
  2. Take the phone out of its case and lay it face up, flat and lengthwise on the lower half of your breastbone. Put the bottom edge (where the microphone is) slightly toward your left side.
  3. Quiet room, phone on Do Not Disturb. Breathe normally, don't talk, keep still.
  4. Tap Start, settle during the 10-second countdown, and stay still until the two beeps. 60 seconds is enough; 2 minutes gives steadier HRV.
  5. Tag anything unusual (coffee, alcohol, short sleep, workout, feeling unwell). Tags explain outliers later.
  6. Mark 3–5 calm, typical mornings as your baseline. Until you do, the first three good readings are used.

What the signals can tell you

  • Heart rate and beat timing. Each beat jolts the chest (seismocardiography, from the accelerometer) and rocks it slightly (gyrocardiography, from the gyroscope). The microphone hears the first and second heart sounds (S1, S2). When both agree, the reading is more trustworthy.
  • Heart rate variability (RMSSD). How much consecutive beats differ. Higher than your usual generally means rested; a sharp drop often follows poor sleep, alcohol, hard training or the start of an illness.
  • Breathing rate, regularity and pauses. The phone tilts as your chest rises, so the gravity reading traces each breath.
  • Breath-linked heart swing. Your heart speeds up on the in-breath and slows on the out-breath (respiratory sinus arrhythmia). A healthy, relaxed system shows a clear swing.
  • Rhythm regularity and extra beats. Large jumps between consecutive beats, and early beats followed by a pause, show up in the beat-interval chart and the Poincaré plot. A steady rhythm makes a tight comet shape; an irregular one scatters.
  • Heart sound timing. The S1 to S2 gap approximates how long each contraction lasts (systole). It shortens as heart rate rises. In practice the iPhone microphone filters out most of the low rumble of heart sounds, so this often stays blank; it hears your breathing clearly instead, which double-checks the breathing rate.
  • Beat shape. The averaged chest-motion waveform has peaks linked to valves opening and closing. Research groups use it for cardiac timing and strength. Here it is compared against your baseline shape; most changes come from phone placement.

What it cannot do: blood pressure, oxygen, an electrical ECG, or reliable murmur detection. A phone microphone is not a stethoscope.

Signs it can graph

These are wellness flags measured against your own baseline, not diagnoses.

Resting heart rate up
Short sleep, alcohol, dehydration, stress, oncoming illness or fever
Shows when: 8+ bpm above baseline, or over 100
HRV down
Same as above; often the earliest sign of being run down
Shows when: About a third below baseline
Breathing faster
Illness, anxiety, fever, lung or heart strain
Shows when: 3+ breaths/min above baseline, or over 20
Irregular rhythm
Often harmless; persistent irregularity can be atrial fibrillation
Shows when: Many big beat-to-beat jumps, in a clean recording
Extra beats
Premature beats; common with caffeine, stress, fatigue
Shows when: 3 or more in a reading
Long gap between beats
A missed or blocked beat, or a recording dropout
Shows when: A gap over 2 s
Breathing pause
Breath-holding or a pause in breathing
Shows when: No breathing motion for 10 s or more
Beat shape changed
Usually a different phone position; occasionally a real change
Shows when: Low match with the baseline waveform
Chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or a racing heart that won't settle needs a doctor or emergency services, not a recording. If irregular rhythm keeps showing up, check it with an ECG (Apple Watch ECG app, KardiaMobile, or your doctor).

Who else is doing this

  • University of Turku and Precordior (Finland) built smartphone mechanocardiography: a phone on the chest, accelerometer plus gyroscope, machine learning on top. Their 2018 study reported about 98% accuracy for detecting atrial fibrillation (Iftikhar et al., Scientific Reports).
  • CardioSignal is the resulting app, aimed at detecting heart conditions from a one-minute phone-on-chest reading, EU-funded (CORDIS project).
  • REFLECS heart-failure study: 1,003 people, a phone on the sternum for 2 minutes; it recognised heart failure with an AUC of 0.95, and the gyroscope beat the accelerometer (JACC: Heart Failure, 2024; trial record).
  • Related consumer devices: digital stethoscopes such as Eko analyse heart sounds; smartwatches flag irregular rhythm and breathing disturbances from wrist sensors.

How your data is kept

Everything stays on this phone. Raw sensor data from your last 10 readings is kept so you can send it to Claude; older raw data is dropped automatically, but the numbers and charts stay. Use Sessions → Save file to keep a backup or to attach your readings in the Claude chat.
